# Evochora: A Simulation Platform for Digital Evolution Research

## Abstract

Evochora is an open-source platform for digital evolution research in which a program is a body. The environment of its organisms is a space made of typed matter, and their logic and behavior are made of the same. They find their energy there, build and place their offspring there, and reshape their surroundings. A neighbor can dismantle them molecule by molecule. Survival is thermodynamic: every instruction costs energy and produces entropy. Harvesting replenishes energy, and only what the organism writes into the environment carries entropy away. The organism dies when it runs out of energy or fills up with entropy. No fitness function rewards it from outside. The platform is built to study what only a body of matter has: material, shape, territory, metabolism, and what of these a parent passes on. The question behind them is what an environment must provide for complexity to keep arising.

The first experiment records are published and citable. In them, each selective sweep was traced to the change that caused it, down to the single molecule. None of the evolved bodies or behaviors were designed or rewarded: parents came to build their children far away, a dispersal that loosened the kin structure of the population; the shell around a child changed to cheaper matter; and the rule for when to reproduce settled in opposite directions in two runs, one with plentiful energy and one with little.

Researchers can set the conditions of a run: the environment's dimensions and landscape, its energy supply, the thermodynamics, and the structure-aware mutation plugins. They write the ancestors it begins from in EvoASM, Evochora's spatial assembly language, from scratch or from a library of behaviors. A run can start from one ancestor or from several that compete. A web visualizer follows a selected organism instruction by instruction, and run data are exported as Parquet files for standard analysis tools. Every run is deterministic, runs at millions of instructions per second on a workstation, has reached billions of ticks, and can be resumed or re-recorded in any detail.

## 1. Introduction

Biology shows that evolution can produce complexity, but also that this takes conditions which were a long time coming: life on Earth remained microbial for most of its history before complex organisms appeared, late and in bursts. Digital evolution has reproduced the early part of this history. In Tierra, the system the field was built on, self-replicating programs gave rise to parasites and to defenses against them within a few million instructions, without any fitness measure imposed from outside [(Ray, 1991)](#ref-ray-1991). In Avida, the field's main experimental system since, complex functions evolve when the environment rewards them, with results that reached the biological literature [(Ofria & Wilke, 2004)](#ref-ofria-2004).

The later part has not been reproduced yet: after an initial burst of innovation, the rate at which new adaptations appear levels off in the systems Bedau and colleagues measured [(Bedau et al., 1998)](#ref-bedau-1998). What is missing has been debated for three decades under the name of open-ended evolution. Several of the conditions proposed concern the organism's body: that it should consist of the same material as its environment, that energy should be something it has to collect rather than processor time it is given, and that it should have to maintain itself against entropy [(Taylor, 2014](#ref-taylor-2014), [2015)](#ref-taylor-2015). Both Tierra and Avida were built around the program. In Tierra an organism is a sequence of instructions in a shared memory that contains nothing else, in Avida a program running on a virtual CPU of its own, and a body in this sense was not part of either design. Artificial chemistries [(Dittrich et al., 2001)](#ref-dittrich-2001) approached the body from the other side. In Squirm3 self-replicating molecules arise from a random soup and matter is conserved, but nothing executes [(Hutton, 2002)](#ref-hutton-2002); in Stringmol, built for questions of this kind, the molecules are programs that act on one another, but a molecule has no extent in space [(Hickinbotham et al., 2016)](#ref-hickinbotham-2016).

Evochora's organisms are programs, they consist of the same material as their environment, and they have extent in space. The material itself does not react; it is what programs read, write and consume. The body is defined by ownership. The environment is a grid, and each of its cells holds one molecule of a few kinds: code, which organisms execute; data, which they read and write; structure, which cannot be passed but only removed at a cost; and energy, which they collect and can store. An organism is the set of cells it owns, and in the environment there is nothing else of it. It has to assemble this body itself. An offspring is not a copy that the system places somewhere, but a structure the parent writes into free cells with its own pointers, at whatever place it can reach. That individuals make such decisions themselves is one of the conditions Soros and Stanley propose for open-endedness [(Soros & Stanley, 2014)](#ref-soros-2014). The child inherits those of these cells the parent has marked for it, together with the energy the parent hands over. Every molecule written costs energy, and what one organism has built, a neighbor can take apart again. The three conditions named above are thus met in the physics: the body is made of the environment's material, which the organism writes out of energy and which is gone when taken up, energy is a molecule the organism has to find and collect, and since instructions produce entropy that only writing into the environment sheds, an organism has to keep changing the environment to stay alive; the idea behind this rule is Schrödinger's [(Schrödinger, 1944)](#ref-schrodinger-1944). Form, size, place, inheritance and the timing of reproduction are decided by the program rather than by the system, and so they are exposed to selection.

These conditions were not designed in one piece. They entered one after another, each when a run showed that something was missing: entropy, for instance, after the first populations had copied over one another into an error catastrophe [(Eigen, 1971)](#ref-eigen-1971). [Energy sources, thermodynamic policies, mutation operators and death handlers](#24-plugin-interfaces) are plugins, components that can be exchanged or added without touching the rest. The question of what an environment has to provide can thereby be asked one condition at a time.

What exists today is a platform on which populations sustain themselves over billions of ticks, and [published runs](PUBLISHED_EXPERIMENTS.md) in which a change of the program swept to fixation and could be traced to its mechanism. The rise of complexity has not been shown in Evochora yet; the platform is built to look for it one condition at a time.

The platform consists of a runtime that runs the simulation, the EvoASM language with its compiler for writing organisms, a data pipeline that records and indexes every run, and a visualizer and an analyzer in the browser. The chapters that follow describe the physics of the environment, the tools for observing and analyzing a run, the first results, the research directions, its reproducibility and the resources a run needs, and the limitations. A running simulation can be watched at [evochora.org](https://evochora.org/). How the project came about is told in the [Origin Story](ORIGIN_STORY.md).

## 2. The Digital Physics of Evochora

In Evochora, the environment is a grid of cells, each holding one molecule. An organism is a virtual machine with an instruction pointer that executes those molecules as code and data pointers that read and write them. Its actions cost energy, which it has to gather, and produce entropy, which it has to shed. The body of an organism is the cells it owns. [Energy sources, thermodynamic policies, mutation operators and death handlers](#24-plugin-interfaces) are configured for each run and define the physics of the environment. This chapter describes these parts in turn, and the ancestor every run begins from.

### 2.1 The Environment and Its Molecules

The environment is an n-dimensional grid. A run configures how many dimensions it has, how large it is in each, and whether its edges wrap around as a torus or bound it. Every cell of the grid holds one molecule. A molecule has a type and a value: the type says what the molecule is to an organism, the value what it carries. There are eight types:

- **CODE**: an instruction of the virtual machine. The value 0 is the NOP instruction and stands for an empty cell.
- **DATA**: a number, used as an argument of instructions.
- **STATE**: a DATA value an organism has written for its own use. It is working memory, not code, so that a value an organism keeps changing can be told from the code it carries. In arithmetic and comparisons it behaves like DATA; Section 2.3 says how a write becomes STATE.
- **ENERGY**: a quantity of energy an organism can take up.
- **STRUCTURE**: an obstacle. Pointers do not pass it, and removing it costs energy.
- **LABEL**: a mark in the code that jumps aim at. Section 2.2 says how a jump finds it.
- **LABELREF**: the argument of a jump, naming the label it aims at.
- **REGISTER**: the argument of an instruction, naming a register of the virtual machine.

All eight are read, written and consumed alike; the last three differ from the first five only in what they mean to a program.

A molecule has one more property, the marker. It belongs to ownership and is introduced with it in Section 2.3.

### 2.2 The Virtual Machine

Each organism runs on a virtual machine of its own. The machine executes the code that lies in the grid and acts on the grid through pointers; it sees nothing of the environment beyond the cells around its pointers. See the [Assembly Specification](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md) for all details of the machine, its instruction set and the language.

### Fig. 1: An organism's virtual machine and its environment
```
   +-------------------------------------------------------------+
   |               Environment: n-dimensional grid               |
   |       [CODE] [DATA] [STATE] [ENERGY] [STRUCTURE] [...]      |
   +--------------^----------------------------^-----------------+
                  |                            |
            execute code             read, write, consume
                  |                            |
   +--------------|------- Virtual machine ----|-----------------+
   |     +--------v--------+        +----------v----------+      |
   |     | Instruction     |        | Data pointers       |      |
   |     | pointer         | ------>| DP0 ... DPn         |      |
   |     +-----------------+        +---------------------+      |
   |         registers, stacks, energy (ER), entropy (SR)        |
   +-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The **instruction pointer** executes an instruction with its arguments and goes on to the next; it skips empty cells. The next instruction lies in the direction of the **direction vector**, which the program can set, so a program is a line of cells through the grid. The **data pointers** are where the organism touches the environment: every molecule it reads, writes or consumes lies at a data pointer or one step from it. A data pointer moves one cell at a time; it cannot jump freely, only to positions the organism has stored before.

The machine has registers for numbers, vectors and positions, in several banks so that procedures can keep values of their own, and three stacks, for data, for calls and for positions; see [Registers](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#registers) and [Stacks](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#stacks) in the specification.

The machine is built to run damaged code. An instruction checks its operands only as far as its execution requires and otherwise reads what it finds; one that cannot execute fails, costs an energy penalty, and the organism goes on with the next ([Error Penalty](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#energy-register-er)). Mutation produces code nobody wrote, and this is what lets such code do something rather than nothing.

Jumps do not target addresses. A jump names a **label**, and the machine searches the grid for a LABEL molecule with that value. The search tolerates small differences, so a label whose value has mutated is still found; and it can reach into the code of other organisms, so one organism can execute another's code. How tolerant the search is and how far it reaches is set for each run; see [Fuzzy label matching](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#fuzzy-label-matching) in the specification.

### 2.3 Ownership and Thermodynamics

An organism owns cells of the grid, and the cells it owns are its body. Empty cells belong to nobody. A cell becomes the organism's when it writes a molecule into it. Its data pointers can only move through empty cells and through its own cells; cells of other organisms they cannot enter. What an organism can do with a neighboring cell is inspect its molecule or take the molecule up, which empties the cell and ends the neighbor's ownership of it. When an organism dies, all its cells become free; what happens to the molecules in them decides a death handler, a plugin described in [Section 2.4](#24-plugin-interfaces). Details are in the specification under [Ownership](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#ownership).

Ownership also decides what a child inherits. When an organism reproduces, the child receives cells from the parent's body and energy from the parent's account. The parent controls which cells: it can mark the molecules it writes, and the marked cells are the ones that pass to the child. It can mark a wall of STRUCTURE around its body, ENERGY it has put aside in its cells, or a copy of its code, and it leaves unmarked what it wants to keep. A DATA value written without a mark becomes STATE, the organism's private working memory, and is never inherited. Details are in the specification under [Molecule Marker](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#molecule-marker). Which of the inherited cells count as the organism's genome is a definition for the analysis, not a rule of the physics ([Analyzing a Run](#33-analyzing-a-run)).

Everything an organism does costs it something, in two accounts, the energy register (ER) and the entropy register (SR): instructions cost energy and produce entropy. An organism gathers energy from the environment and sheds entropy by writing molecules into it. It dies when its energy is used up or its entropy exceeds the limit. So an organism that only takes up and destroys dies of its entropy, while one that builds can live on. The idea comes from Schrödinger, who described living systems as keeping themselves up by passing entropy on to their surroundings [(Schrödinger, 1944)](#ref-schrodinger-1944). What an instruction costs is set by thermodynamic policies, which are configured for a run; for reading and writing, a policy can depend on the type and value of the molecule and on whether the cell is the organism's own, foreign or unowned. Details are in the specification under [Thermodynamic Configuration](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#thermodynamic-configuration).

In Evochora's default configuration, taking up an ENERGY molecule credits its value to the energy account. Writing a molecule lowers the entropy account, with two exceptions: STATE, because it is the organism's own memory and not something built into the environment, and ENERGY, because the organism can take it back unchanged. Taking a molecule up from a foreign cell costs far more than from an own or unowned cell, so that taking a neighbor's body apart is expensive; ENERGY is the exception and credits its value whoever owns the cell. A run can set each of these differently.

### 2.4 Plugin Interfaces

The physics is modular, so that its parts can themselves be the subject of an experiment. For each of the following aspects there is a plugin interface, with standard plugins that come with Evochora; a different behavior can be implemented in Java.

**Resource distribution** — How does energy enter the environment? Built-in models include continuous probabilistic spawning (analogous to solar radiation), periodic eruptions at fixed locations (geysers), one-time seeding, and vaults of energy behind walls of STRUCTURE that are refilled. A run can replace or combine them. All of them are tick plugins, which run once every tick with full access to the environment and the organisms, so anything that changes the environment over time, a landscape, a season, a catastrophe, can be written as one.

**Thermodynamic costs** — What does an action cost? Thermodynamic policies set the energy and entropy of each instruction, and for reading and writing they can depend on the molecule and on who owns the cell ([Section 2.3](#23-ownership-and-thermodynamics)). A run can change them or use new ones.

**Mutation** — What happens to an organism's genome during reproduction? Four built-in mutation operators insert, substitute, delete and duplicate code. They are structure-aware: they read the body as the machine would, so what they insert is executable and what they duplicate or delete is a whole block of code. A run can configure, combine or replace them.

**Death and recycling** — What happens when an organism dies? The standard plugin replaces a dead organism's molecules with one configured molecule. By default that is the empty cell, so the body disappears; configured as ENERGY, the dead become food for others.

**Label matching** — How does a jump find its label? The standard strategy matches by Hamming distance, own labels first ([Section 2.2](#22-the-virtual-machine)), and passes label values unchanged from parent to child, so that relatives share their addresses; an optional namespace flip at birth lets lineages drift apart in them.

**Instruction interception** — An interface for changing instructions before they execute, for example to let a region of the environment corrupt them like radiation. No standard plugin uses it yet.

### 2.5 The Ancestor

An ancestor is a self-replicating program written by hand, and a run starts from one or several of them. Three come with Evochora, in [`assembly/ancestors`](../assembly/ancestors). The default one is put together from the [library of behaviors](../assembly/lib/behavior), reusable modules an organism can be built from, and does three things in a loop. It harvests: a data pointer walks through the surroundings and takes up the energy it finds. It hoards: it keeps a store of energy inside its body, filled in good times and drawn on when energy runs low. And it reproduces when it has energy to spare or entropy to shed: it builds the child's shell of STRUCTURE first, then the body row by row, marks both for the child, and gives up when the place for the shell turns out to be taken by someone else. A flag makes it aggressive instead: it then takes up what lies in the way, as long as its energy and entropy allow. The other two ancestors are earlier designs kept for comparison; the header of each program says what it does. What an ancestor brings along is the starting point of a run, not a bound on what evolves in it: a run can seed another ancestor, or several, and leaves the rest to evolution.

## 3. Running Experiments

An experiment in Evochora is a configuration file and one or more ancestors. This chapter describes how a run is set up and started, how it is observed, how its data are analyzed, and how a run is continued or recorded again.

### 3.1 Setting Up a Run

A run is described by a configuration file. The file that comes with Evochora, [`config/evochora.conf`](../config/evochora.conf), is the template: copy it, change what the experiment needs, and start Evochora with it, as the [Quick Start](../README.md#quick-start) of the README and the [CLI usage](CLI_USAGE.md#start-simulation-node) describe. The file sets the environment, its dimensions, size and topology; the ancestors, each with its program, its starting position and its initial energy; the plugins ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)) with their options; and the seed. A program can take settings from the configuration as well, as [flags](CLI_USAGE.md#flags---define) and [values](CLI_USAGE.md#values---value). Every setting is documented where it is defined, in the [reference configuration](../src/main/resources/reference.conf).

A run records a sample of its ticks. A recording profile sets the sampling interval, the number of ticks between two recorded states, and nothing downstream sees finer than that: the standard profiles record every tick, every hundredth or every ten-thousandth. The interval is a choice between detail and cost. An environment of millions of cells recorded every tick fills storage fast, and writing it slows the simulation down until the disk, not the processor, sets the pace. Long runs in large environments are therefore usually recorded sparsely, and a stretch that turns out to matter can be recorded again in detail afterwards ([Resuming and Re-recording a Run](#34-resuming-and-re-recording-a-run)).

### 3.2 Observing a Run

The visualizer shows a run in the browser, recorded tick by recorded tick, while the run is going on or afterwards. It draws the environment as a two-dimensional grid; a run in more dimensions is recorded and analyzed like any other; only the drawing is two-dimensional. For a selected organism it shows the registers, the stacks, energy and entropy, the instruction being executed and the next one, and the line of the ancestor's source this code came from, where the code has not mutated. The mutations in the organism's lineage are marked in the grid, and its ancestry is drawn as a strip from the founder to its parent. A public instance at [evochora.org](https://evochora.org/) shows a recorded run.

![The visualizer: the environment with organisms, and the state of a selected organism](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/3bf0aa8e-f2bd-4b98-a03f-dd33d449ed93)

For long runs, the `video` command renders the recorded data into a film ([CLI usage](CLI_USAGE.md#render-simulation-videos)).

### 3.3 Analyzing a Run

The analyzer, also in the browser, shows the metrics of a run as charts over its whole length: how the population develops, how organisms are born and die and of what, how genomes change and spread, which instructions are executed and which fail, and what the environment consists of. Its data are the metrics that analytics plugins compute from the recorded states and export as Parquet files, the standard columnar format of data science; a run can add plugins of its own, and their metrics appear beside the others.

![The analyzer: charts of a run's metrics](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/eb83082c-0a04-4572-aaa3-2e601a1322c3)

Analysis is not limited to these tools. The same Parquet files can be loaded into Python or R, into a Jupyter notebook or into DuckDB, where researchers can apply their own methods: phylogenetic reconstruction, diversity indices, survival analysis, genome comparison, or whatever their field uses. A [Jupyter notebook](https://colab.research.google.com/github/evochora/evochora/blob/main/notebooks/data_analysis_guide.ipynb) demonstrates this with phylogenetic trees, Muller plots and cross-metric analysis using pandas, networkx and DuckDB.

Phylogenies, Muller plots and genome comparison all rest on one question: what counts as a genome. Organisms in Evochora have no genome in the biological sense: there is no separation of genotype and phenotype, the body is the program, and what a child inherits is whatever its parent marked for it. The platform does not define a genome; it records what an organism is born with. For analysis, a genome is therefore an operational definition: the cells a newborn owns, normalized for its label namespace (see label matching under [Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)), minus the molecules a run declares to be the organism's own work rather than its inheritance. By default these are its STATE cells and the STRUCTURE shell the ancestor builds around every child: both differ between copies of one genome without a change in the code, and counting them would make such copies look like mutants. The definition is a parameter of the run and is recorded with it. Within it, a change in any cell, damage included, counts as a change of genome. A separation of genotype and phenotype, a region of data that steers the behavior, read by logic the mutation operators do not touch, is something an organism could implement itself; whether it evolves or is designed is an experimental question, not a rule of the environment.

The data are open to any method of the field. The possible analyses range from phylogenetic trees and Muller plots to fitness landscapes and complexity measures such as assembly theory [(Sharma et al., 2023)](#ref-sharma-2023); the platform delivers the data in a form that lets researchers bring their own methods to each.

### 3.4 Resuming and Re-recording a Run

A run that was stopped can be continued with `node resume` from a recorded state near its end. The ticks between that state and the stop are computed again, identically ([Reproducibility](#61-reproducibility)), so a sparse recording loses nothing of the run. A stretch of a run can be recorded again with `node fork`: Evochora replays the run from a recorded state before the chosen tick and records the stretch as a new run, with a sampling interval of its own, every tick if needed. This is how an event found in the sparse record of a long run can be examined in detail ([CLI usage](CLI_USAGE.md#fork-a-run-node-fork)).

## 4. First Results

The project publishes its results as experiment records, packages on Zenodo under a CC0 license. A record holds the run's recorded data, the build that wrote them, and an executed notebook with the question, the evidence, the interpretation and the limits. Anyone can read the finding, run the analysis again, browse the run in the visualizer without the project's servers, and execute the run again from any stored state with the same result ([Reproducibility](#61-reproducibility)). [Published Experiments](PUBLISHED_EXPERIMENTS.md) lists the records with their DOIs. This chapter says what they show about the platform.

The populations behind these findings sustained themselves for hundreds of millions to billions of ticks. The organisms in them were given no task and no reward. Lineages still arose that built differently from their ancestor and replaced the population. Nothing in the system was written to favor them, and they spread faster than the neutral expectation the records compute. Where a child is built, what it is made of and when a parent reproduces are decisions of the program, and in these runs evolution changed all three. A lineage came to build its children far from the parent, and the kin structure of the population loosened. Another changed the material of the shell around its children to a cheaper one. The same rule for when to reproduce settled in opposite directions in two runs, under plentiful and under scarce energy; whether the energy supply alone decides it is a question for an experiment.

Because the body is the program and every cell of it is recorded, the cause of each sweep could be read off the run itself, down to the single molecule. The record of the shell shows how far that reaches. An inserted instruction removed one cell of the organism's own body during a harvest. The machine read the missing operand as another register, one that held the energy level ([The Virtual Machine](#22-the-virtual-machine)). The loop that builds the shell took its material from that register and wrote the shell at a twentieth of the price. Each step stands in the recorded execution. A rule written for one purpose was used by evolution for another, as rules have been in digital systems since their beginning [(Lehman et al., 2020)](#ref-lehman-2020). A record can carry the measures the field uses. The record of the far-built children measures evolutionary activity after Bedau, Snyder and Packard [(Bedau et al., 1998)](#ref-bedau-1998), the test of whether new adaptations keep arising.

These findings are observations, not experiments. The runs behind them were made to test the platform while it was built; none was designed around a question, and the sweeps were found afterwards. What a trace shows is the mechanism; that the mechanism caused the advantage is a further claim. Other changes rode along with the ones that mattered, and where the carrier and the cause arose in one organism, observation alone cannot separate them. An experiment could separate them: one run seeded with an ancestor that carries the change, beside one seeded with the ancestor without it. The records of these findings therefore say "consistent with selection" and no more. Whether any organism became more complex was not measured. In the run that was measured, evolutionary activity stayed bounded, the class in which Bedau and colleagues found the artificial systems of their time; whether a run can be made to raise it is an open question. Measured in generations the runs are short, a few thousand at most with the ancestors used. [Limitations](#7-limitations) returns to these limits. What a run designed around a question could show is the subject of the next chapter.

## 5. Research Directions

A research direction here is a question that Evochora's physics makes askable. Some can be put as experiments today, from a configuration and ancestors; others would first need a change to the physics, and each section says which. The sections are ordered from the first kind toward the second. They are examples; the same parts, energy sources, thermodynamic policies, death handlers, mutation plugins, label matching and the ancestor's library of behaviors ([The Ancestor](#25-the-ancestor)), can be combined into experiments beyond them.

Behind all of them stands the question the introduction began with: whether complexity keeps arising, and what an environment has to provide for it. The field measures it with Bedau's statistics of evolutionary activity, which levelled off in the systems Bedau and colleagues measured [(Bedau et al., 1998)](#ref-bedau-1998), and the workshop in York asked that the observable hallmarks of a system be kept apart from the mechanisms thought to produce them [(Taylor et al., 2016)](#ref-taylor-2016).

Evochora's answer is this chapter: the conditions the field suspects are plugins and ancestors here, added or removed one at a time, so that a run can ask which condition makes which hallmark respond. The abundance and the descent of every genome are in the data, so the field's measures can be computed from a run ([Analyzing a Run](#33-analyzing-a-run)).

### 5.1 Replicator Ecology

A replicator ecology arises when several kinds of self-replicating programs live in one environment on the same supply of resources. Can two strategies that live on the same resource coexist, or does one exclude the other? What does space change? When does conflict pay? Theory has answers that experiments with living organisms can test only indirectly. Where individuals stay in place and meet only their neighbors, peaceful strategies can survive in patches that a mixed population would lose [(Nowak & May, 1992)](#ref-nowak-may-1992). Whether aggression pays depends on the cost of a fight set against the value of what is fought over, and a population often settles on a mixture of strategies [(Maynard Smith & Price, 1973)](#ref-maynard-smith-price-1973); in nature, the cost of a fight cannot be set.

Digital systems have produced such ecologies. In Tierra, an ecology of parasites, immune hosts and hyperparasites arose within a few million instructions [(Ray, 1991)](#ref-ray-1991) ([Host–Parasite Coevolution](#53-hostparasite-coevolution)). In Avida, predators were added as an extension ([Predators and Prey](#52-predators-and-prey)). In Evochora, strategies compete for energy and for space in one currency. Harvesting, hoarding, a child's body and its shell all cost energy, and so does aggression: taking up a cell that belongs to a neighbor costs what the run's thermodynamic policy sets for it. Defense is construction, paid before any attacker comes. Organisms meet only as neighbors, and a body blocks the pointers of others, so a place is a resource too.

Evochora can turn these questions into experiments through its ancestors and its configuration. A run can be seeded with several strategies, and the cost of a fight is a setting of the run. Since the default ancestor is assembled from a library of behaviors ([The Ancestor](#25-the-ancestor)), a strategy can be a variant of it: a behavior exchanged, a flag set, a threshold changed ([Setting Up a Run](#31-setting-up-a-run)), as in its aggressive variant. Conceivable experiments start with two strategies on the same energy supply, to see whether they coexist or one excludes the other, and whether the density and the arrangement of the energy sources decide it. The cost of aggression can be varied between runs, to find the price at which aggressors lose, and the shell the ancestor builds around each child can be tested in populations with and without aggressors, to see whether the defense pays. Long runs can show whether evolution alters the strategies or makes them converge. Every organism's record names the ancestor program it descends from, so the fate of each strategy can be read from the data, and the visualizer colors organisms by lineage. An experiment of this kind can be set up today from a configuration and its ancestors. The analytics count the population by genome, so comparing strategies is a query on the recorded data; a metric by ancestor program, a small addition, would make it a chart of the analyzer.

Coexistence through niches lies one step further. Two strategies that live on different things need a second kind of energy or a molecule only some can use, which is the subject of [Metabolism and Food Webs](#59-metabolism-and-food-webs).

### 5.2 Predators and Prey

Where one organism lives on another, their numbers chase each other: predators grow while prey is plentiful and starve when they have eaten it down, and the cycles that Lotka and Volterra wrote down for this are among the oldest results of theoretical ecology [(Lotka, 1925](#ref-lotka-1925); [Volterra, 1926)](#ref-volterra-1926). Digital evolution has reached them by construction: in Avida, predators that consume other organisms were introduced as an extension, and the prey evolved to escape them [(Wagner et al., 2013)](#ref-wagner-2013).

In Evochora a body is matter, and matter has a price. Today a cell of CODE or DATA costs little to write, and taking one from a neighbor costs the taker energy and entropy and yields nothing but the place; only stored ENERGY yields its value to a thief ([Replicator Ecology](#51-replicator-ecology)). A run can change that with the thermodynamic policy alone ([Ownership and Thermodynamics](#23-ownership-and-thermodynamics)): it can make such a cell expensive to write and credit whoever takes a foreign one with somewhat less than it cost. Then no energy is created anywhere, a body is worth about what a child costs, and a body is prey. STRUCTURE keeps its price, so a shell stays what it is, armor that a predator has to pay to breach. A body whose code is damaged runs differently, in most cases worse or not at all.

What this needs is a predator: an ancestor that searches for bodies the way the harvester searches for energy, breaks a shell and takes what lies behind it. Beside it a prey, the harvester as it is, perhaps with a behavior that spends on its shell what a predator would have to pay to get through; both with their decisions, when to go out and when to reproduce, set to the prices of such a world. The experiment is their numbers over time, and whether they cycle.

One question comes before all others. Nothing in Evochora tells an organism whether the body beside it is kin, and the ancestors build their children beside themselves, so a predator would eat its own descendants first. Whether a predator lineage can persist at all, and whether it comes to spare its kin, is a question of kin recognition, and a new instruction that tells kin from stranger would be the one addition this direction asks of the platform.

### 5.3 Host–Parasite Coevolution

Parasites appear wherever a replicator can use another's machinery. In Tierra they arose in the first runs: programs that had lost their copy routine and used a neighbor's, hosts immune to them, and hyperparasites that turned the parasites' processing to their own reproduction [(Ray, 1991)](#ref-ray-1991). In Stringmol they arise almost at once. A mixed population dies of them, while a spatial one forms waves in which hosts and parasites pursue one another and both persist; under that pursuit the replicators evolve ways to tell their own copies from parasites, the parasites evolve around them, and when parasites become rare the hosts shed their defenses again [(Hickinbotham et al., 2021)](#ref-hickinbotham-2021). In Avida, where every organism runs on a processor of its own, parasites had to be introduced; hosts that coevolved with them developed more complex functions and more evolvable genomes than hosts without them [(Zaman et al., 2014)](#ref-zaman-2014). Parasitism is among the few mechanisms for which a drive toward complexity has been shown in digital systems.

In Evochora, how a jump finds its label is a plugin of the run. The standard label matching ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)) takes a neighbor's label only when none of the organism's own matches. The instruction pointer executes what stands in a cell, whoever owns it; the data pointers never enter another's cells ([Ownership and Thermodynamics](#23-ownership-and-thermodynamics)), so a neighbor's body can be read only from outside, and written into only by exchanging a cell at its edge. Organisms that jump to a neighbor's label and execute its code have been observed in many runs. Under the standard strategy this happens when an organism's own label is missing or changed, by a flawed copy or by a mutation, and what it executes then is a relative's routine in place of the one it lost.

Whether that becomes a way of life is the question. A lineage that lives on a neighbor's routine needs a relative with matching labels within reach, generation after generation; whether it can keep one, and what follows if it does, waves, defenses and their loss as in Stringmol, is open. The conditions are settings of a run: the reach and tolerance of the label search, where the ancestor places its children, the price of a foreign read, and the namespace flip, a plugin option that changes every label of a newborn by one bit, so that the addresses of lineages drift apart with the generations and out of a parasite's tolerance. Which organism executed whose code stands in the record at every recorded tick, as the position of its instruction pointer against the owner of the cell; counted over a whole run, it would be a metric, computed by a query on the record or by a new analytics plugin.

Two things lie beyond what arises by itself. A parasite ancestor could be written by hand with the system as it is: a program that exchanges cells of a neighbor's body for its own, so that the neighbor's instruction pointer executes logic that serves the parasite, paid with the neighbor's energy. The second would require an instruction added to the system: a jump whose tolerance and reach the program sets itself would place the search under selection, and whether selection widens it toward exploitation or narrows it is a question only such an instruction can put.

### 5.4 Heterogeneous and Dynamic Environments

Biology explains much of diversification by the structure of the environment. Populations separated by a barrier diverge [(Mayr, 1963)](#ref-mayr-1963), gradients of resources make specialists, and after a catastrophe the emptied space fills with radiations of new forms [(Schluter, 2000)](#ref-schluter-2000). In nature all of this is observed after the fact, and in the laboratory over short spans and in simple environments, as when a bacterium diversified into niche specialists within days in an unshaken flask [(Rainey & Travisano, 1998)](#ref-rainey-travisano-1998). In a simulation the structure is a choice. Avida has resources that can be confined to places and an event system that disturbs a run at set times, with extinctions or changed mutation rates [(Ofria & Wilke, 2004)](#ref-ofria-2004).

In Evochora, the structure of the environment is matter. A barrier can be a wall of STRUCTURE molecules, and crossing it costs what the run charges for taking a cell apart, so isolation has a price rather than a rule. A gradient, a season or a zone of energy that drifts across the environment can be made of where and when ENERGY molecules are placed. A lineage passes a gap only by building its children on the other side, so migration can be read from the record as the spread of a lineage, cell by cell. The dimensions, size and topology of the grid, a torus or a bounded box, are settings of a run.

Conceivable experiments start with one ancestor on both sides of a wall, to see whether isolation alone makes the lineages diverge in what they do, not only in their genomes, and whether one side displaces the other once the wall is opened. Islands joined by narrow corridors can show whether they hold a diversity an open environment loses. A region emptied at a chosen tick can show how populations return, and whether the return depends on their mutation regime or their strategy. A gradient of energy can show whether sparing strategies settle the poor part and fast ones the rich.

The energy sources that come with Evochora place energy evenly at random, at fixed points at intervals, or in vaults behind walls ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)), and size, dimensions and topology are configuration. Walls, regions, gradients, seasons and catastrophes would each be a small tick plugin ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)), and a landscape that changes over time needs no more than one of them. One idea goes further, a molecule a pointer cannot simply take apart: the instruction that tries fails, and the molecule loses one of its value with every attempt until it dissolves at zero, or never, if its value is negative. A barrier would then be measured in attempts rather than in energy, and an experiment could separate the two.

### 5.5 The Structure of Variation

What evolution can find depends on what variation offers it. The capacity of a system to produce useful variation, its evolvability, is itself shaped by evolution [(Wagner, 2005)](#ref-wagner-2005), and forces that select nothing, drift and mutation pressure, shape the architecture of genomes [(Lynch, 2007)](#ref-lynch-2007). Digital systems make the structure of variation a setting. Avida has copy mutations that always yield a legal instruction, insertions and deletions at division, point mutations in running programs, and implicit mutations from a parent's flawed copy algorithm, which an experimenter cannot control but can suppress [(Ofria & Wilke, 2004)](#ref-ofria-2004). Aevol models genomes as sequences with genes and non-coding stretches and applies the full spectrum of rearrangements, duplication, deletion, inversion and translocation, at rates per base [(Batut et al., 2013)](#ref-batut-2013). In Stringmol only point mutations are defined, and yet the evolving replicators change their own mutation rates and produce larger changes by mechanisms of their own [(Hickinbotham et al., 2021)](#ref-hickinbotham-2021).

In Evochora, variation has two channels. The first is the copy itself: a parent writes its child cell by cell, and the copy can be incomplete, or a neighbor can have taken or overwritten cells of it before the birth. The write of a cell is exact, but the routine that copies is part of the body and inherited with it, so what it skips, doubles or displaces is exposed to mutation and selection like any other trait, as the replication strategies of Stringmol are. One of the runs described in [First Results](#4-first-results) shows this channel at work: two labels lost during a copy were inherited and became part of the change that spread. The second is the mutation plugins, which act on the newborn's body at birth; the four that come with Evochora insert, substitute, delete and duplicate ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)). They read the body as the machine does, so what they insert is executable and what they duplicate or delete is a whole block between labels; they know the syntax of a body, not its function, and evaluate nothing. Their rates and biases are settings: by default, deletion takes only a block whose label the body carries twice, so it removes what a duplication left, though both copies may have changed in the meantime, and substitution weights each molecule type for how exposed it is. One of the runs described in [First Results](#4-first-results) shows what that weight does: when a lineage changed the material of its shell from a type the substitution never touches to one it does, a quarter of all substitutions moved from the code into the shell. How neutral a mutation of a label is, the label search decides: its tolerance is the width of the neutral network for label mutations. Every birth is sorted by what made its genome, an unchanged copy, a flawed one, or the events of which operator, and every cell an operator changed is recorded ([Analyzing a Run](#33-analyzing-a-run)), so the fate of each class of variation can be read against births and offspring.

Conceivable experiments vary the rates of the operators one at a time and together. Deletion can be let loose on unique blocks, to compare deletion confined to duplicated blocks with deletion of any block. The plugins can be switched off entirely, to see what the copy channel alone sustains, the mirror image of Avida's option, which suppresses the implicit mutations and keeps the explicit ones. The tolerance of the label search can be widened, to see whether populations cross valleys that stricter matching bars. And the robustness of a program against each class of variation can be measured, because the analytics sort every birth by class.

All of this is configuration; the operators are plugins of the birth with full access to the newborn's body, and others can be written against the same interface. Three ideas lie beyond them. Mutation during life rather than at birth, Avida's cosmic ray, would be an interceptor plugin, for which the interface exists but no plugin yet. Copy errors at the instruction that writes a cell, rather than at the child, would make mutation part of the copy itself. And a mutation rate that depends on the organism's own energy or entropy would give Evochora a counterpart of stress-induced mutagenesis, where bacteria raise their mutation rate when they are poorly adapted [(Galhardo et al., 2007)](#ref-galhardo-2007); whether such a rule helps a population out of a poor environment or hastens its end is a question that rule would allow.

### 5.6 Genotype and Phenotype

In biology, hereditary information and the body it describes are separate, and the machinery between them, which reads the one and builds the other, is itself a product of evolution. Von Neumann held such a separation, a description and a constructor, to be the condition for complexity to evolve [(von Neumann, 1966)](#ref-von-neumann-1966); Tierra showed that a program which copies itself instruction by instruction evolves without it [(Ray, 1991)](#ref-ray-1991). Taylor names the catch: where the machinery that reads the genotype cannot evolve, as Tierra's processor cannot, evolution cannot find new ways of representing a phenotype [(Taylor, 2014)](#ref-taylor-2014). That a separation can evolve has been shown in Stringmol, where description, copier and constructor evolved together from a chemistry that had none [(Clark et al., 2017)](#ref-clark-2017). Aevol has a designed and explicit map from sequence to function [(Batut et al., 2013)](#ref-batut-2013), Flow-Lenia carries the parameters of its rule in the mass of a creature [(Plantec et al., 2023)](#ref-plantec-2023), and many simulations of evolution vary a vector of traits and nothing else. Under what conditions a separation arises, and what it changes, is open. Evochora leaves it to the organism: the platform records what a newborn owns and does not say which part of it is genotype, which is why a genome is a definition of the analysis ([Analyzing a Run](#33-analyzing-a-run)), and why the separation is an experiment.

In Evochora, the body is the program, and the numbers in a program are DATA cells: every threshold, every child's dowry, every width stands as a literal in an instruction. The substitution plugin weights molecules by type and, on top, by the slot they stand in, literals above the rest ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)). With the weights of CODE and LABEL at zero and that of DATA above it, a run varies only the numbers and leaves the logic alone: the evolution of traits that simple simulations perform, by configuration, with the full variation beside it for comparison. One step further, an ancestor can keep its parameters in one region of its body, read by its logic, and a plugin can confine variation to that region. The organism then has a designed genotype, and what reads it is not the machine but its own code; the machine beneath stays fixed, in Evochora as in Tierra, but the layer above it can evolve.

Conceivable experiments set the evolution of traits against the full variation, to see which adapts faster, which stays stable, and whether the full variation finds behavior that the variation of traits cannot. A designed genotype can hold the size of the shell as one of its fields, to see whether size responds to selection when only it varies. All of this is configuration and an ancestor; the plugin for a region would be a small addition.

### 5.7 A Digital Eukaryogenesis

Life on Earth stayed small and simple for most of its history, and one explanation is energetic. Lane and Martin argue from the energy a cell can spend per gene: more genes and more proteins per cell were, for a prokaryote, more than it could pay for, and the step to complex cells came when energy production moved inside, with the mitochondria, engulfed bacteria that kept their own membranes and part of their own genome [(Lane & Martin, 2010)](#ref-lane-martin-2010). The hypothesis is contested [(Lynch & Marinov, 2015)](#ref-lynch-marinov-2015). The part of it that has a counterpart in Evochora is geometric: a cell gains its energy at its membrane, and a membrane grows more slowly than the volume it has to supply. Tierra, Avida and Aevol are built around the program, and a body with an edge is not part of their design, so that part has had no test. In Evochora it has both: an area of cells and an edge.

Today the two sides of the account are uneven. A child costs its parent one write per cell, so the price of a body grows with its area; a larger body carries more code and more behavior, and where that behavior pays, it pays for the copying too. But the intake of energy does not grow with size: energy lies where it falls, and a pointer walks to it. An energy source that places energy alongside an organism's shell, together with a harvest that walks along it, would change that: intake would grow with the perimeter while cost grows with the area, linearly and quadratically with the body's extent, which is the prokaryote's constraint in two dimensions. Whether size and form would then come under selection at all is open. Ancestors of different sizes and shapes can be seeded and compared, to see whether there is a body size no lineage sustains, and what becomes of programs that need more code than their edge can feed. Both pieces, the source and the harvest, would be a plugin ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)) and a behavior ([The Ancestor](#25-the-ancestor)), written against what exists. The analogy could be carried one step further with a cost of upkeep for every cell an organism owns, charged each tick by a plugin, since a cell's volume costs it continuously, while a body in Evochora costs once, when it is copied.

The way out, in the analogy, is to move energy production inside. The biology sits between two designs: a mitochondrion has a body and a reproduction of its own, but most of its machinery comes from the host. In Evochora the simpler half can be built today: a parent can fork a child without a body of its own, whose instruction pointer runs one routine of the parent, a harvest, say, and leaves what it gathers where the parent takes it up, and the parent decides how many such helpers it keeps. Such a helper tests whether an inner supply of energy pays, not how it arose; endosymbiosis began with two lineages. The fuller forms would need a runtime extension: a body within the body, or execution contexts that share ownership with the organism, so that each part could copy itself and the organism divide like a cell. Whether a body with a helper sustains more code than one without, and whether coordination between them can arise from the cells they share or needs instructions of its own, are the questions this direction would put.

### 5.8 Toward Multicellularity

The transitions that made cells into organisms, and organisms into colonies, are the major transitions of evolution [(Maynard Smith & Szathmáry, 1995)](#ref-maynard-smith-1995). The first multicellular forms are thought to have arisen from cells that stayed together after dividing rather than by aggregating [(Tarnita et al., 2013)](#ref-tarnita-2013), long before any of them specialized, and in the laboratory yeast took that step within weeks: cells that stayed attached formed clusters, and the clusters multiplied by breaking apart [(Ratcliff et al., 2012)](#ref-ratcliff-2012). Cooperation among such cells pays where they are kin [(Hamilton, 1964)](#ref-hamilton-1964). Digital systems have studied the later steps with the group given. In Avida, groups that reproduce as a unit evolve a division of labor when switching tasks costs something, and a separation of germ and soma when work is mutagenic [(Goldsby et al., 2012](#ref-goldsby-2012), [2014)](#ref-goldsby-2014). In DISHTINY, cells with signaling and resource sharing built in form kin groups and evolve multicellular strategies [(Moreno & Ofria, 2019](#ref-moreno-2019), [2022)](#ref-moreno-2022).

In Evochora nothing gives a group. The ancestors that come with it build each child beside its parent, so kin lie together, and that is where the first step can be tested. An organism enclosed by its own descendants is in trouble: its data pointers reach no energy outside, it has no room for a child, and it can shed entropy only by writing, which costs energy it cannot replace. A group whose outer members pass energy inward, by laying ENERGY cells where the inner ones take them up, could keep its interior alive; the giver pays what it gives, the taker gains it whole, and the taking costs no entropy ([Ownership and Thermodynamics](#23-ownership-and-thermodynamics)). Whether such a group outgrows one that does not is an experiment of two ancestors and no extension. It would spread as a patch of kin, as the first clusters did; whether it comes to reproduce as a unit is the question. Staying together is itself a trait: the nearness of kin is the ancestor's doing, not a rule of the run, and in one of the runs described in [First Results](#4-first-results) evolution loosened it.

Specialization within such a group is the mechanism of [A Digital Eukaryogenesis](#57-a-digital-eukaryogenesis) applied to kin: a parent that gives its children bodies of their own but one role each, a harvester here, a builder there, has a division of labor by design. That tests whether the division pays, not whether it arises, and an experiment can ask whether a group of specialists outgrows a group of equals. Whether it arises can be asked too, once the ancestors share: a single mutation that takes a child's reproduction and leaves its harvest makes a sterile provider of it, and the record shows whether such providers persist in patches of kin more often than outside them, and whether the patches that have them grow.

Beyond that, three things would have to be added. The instruction that tells kin from stranger, which [Predators and Prey](#52-predators-and-prey) asks for first. A unit above the organism that the platform can represent once organisms form one, the shared ownership named in [A Digital Eukaryogenesis](#57-a-digital-eukaryogenesis), as distinct from a group the experimenter sets up. And a way to send a signal to the members of such a unit: a label value that the receivers answer by calling the label of their own that matches it best, which the label search already knows how to find ([Fuzzy label matching](ASSEMBLY_SPEC.md#fuzzy-label-matching)). With those, the questions of Avida and DISHTINY could be asked without a group being given: whether a group comes to reproduce as a unit, and whether one of its members comes to carry the information for the whole and build it anew, a germ.

### 5.9 Metabolism and Food Webs

In the biosphere matter is conserved, so what one organism leaves behind is what another lives on; food chains, cycles and the interconnection of species follow from that. Taylor names the absence of this as a cause of the low diversity of digital evolution: where a copy arises out of thin air, organisms are not resources for one another, and no food chain can form [(Taylor, 2014)](#ref-taylor-2014). Microbial theory says how such chains come about: a strain that degrades a resource only partly and excretes the intermediate saves enzymes, and a second strain lives on what it excretes [(Pfeiffer & Bonhoeffer, 2004)](#ref-pfeiffer-bonhoeffer-2004). Avida has reactions that consume one resource and produce another, and chains of them, defined by the experimenter, sustain communities of coexisting types [(Ofria & Wilke, 2004)](#ref-ofria-2004). In Evochora, what one organism leaves behind feeds another only where a run turns the dead into energy ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)); the chain this section proposes carries energy, not the matter of bodies.

The idea is a metabolism. Energy would come in substrates, molecules ([The Environment and Its Molecules](#21-the-environment-and-its-molecules)) whose value is a pattern of positions, laid by the energy sources ([Plugin Interfaces](#24-plugin-interfaces)) in patches rather than scattered. An organism takes a substrate into a reservoir of its own and digests it there with the enzymes it inherits: each enzyme answers to one position and releases the energy that position holds, and each costs time and energy as every instruction does, whether it finds its position or not. What the enzymes do not answer to stays in the reservoir as leftover, until the organism excretes it by writing it back into the environment, which, like every write, sheds entropy ([Ownership and Thermodynamics](#23-ownership-and-thermodynamics)). Positions arise only in the sources and vanish only in digestion; nothing else creates or destroys them. The leftover of one organism is a substrate to another whose enzymes answer to the remaining positions, and that is a food chain. An enzyme applied to a position the substrate lacks costs the same and yields nothing, so an organism with many enzymes pays for all of them on every molecule: where each source lays substrates of a few positions of its own, specialists win, where substrates are rich, generalists do, as the theory of optimal foraging predicts [(MacArthur & Pianka, 1966)](#ref-macarthur-pianka-1966). Leftovers that nobody can digest accumulate, and what accumulates becomes the niche of whoever learns to use it [(Odling-Smee et al., 2003)](#ref-odling-smee-2003).

This would add to the platform a molecule type, a reservoir in the virtual machine, instructions for digestion, and sources that lay substrates in patches. The questions a first run could put are whether chains of digesters and digesters of their leftovers arise, at which composition of the supply specialists displace generalists, and whether a world fills with leftovers before anything learns to use them, or whether a slow decay of substrates has to stand in for the weathering that nature provides.

## 6. Reproducibility and Resources

### 6.1 Reproducibility

A run is determined by its seed, its configuration and the build that executes it. With the same three it takes the same course on any machine, cell for cell, on Linux, Windows and macOS alike; a test of the project checks this on all three. A run that was paused and resumed from a stored state continues as an uninterrupted run would have. Randomness is part of the state. The only data of a run that vary between two executions are the wall-clock times of its capture, and they are metadata.

A run is recorded as a sequence of full snapshots with the changes between them. How dense the recording is, every tick or every ten-thousandth, is a setting of the run and the lever between resolution and storage; the [User Guide](USER_GUIDE.md) describes the profiles. Because a run is deterministic, a sparse recording loses nothing for good. Any stretch can be recorded again from the stored state before it, at any density, with the same course ([Record a Stretch in Full](USER_GUIDE.md#6-record-a-stretch-in-full)). This is how the sweeps of [First Results](#4-first-results) were read: the runs were recorded sparsely, and the stretches that mattered were re-recorded instruction by instruction afterwards. The density of the recording does not change the run; it changes what is kept of it. Events are kept whatever the density: every birth, death and mutation is written once, in the next recorded tick, with the newborn's genome and parent. What a sparser recording loses is the state between two recorded ticks, the bodies as they were and the instructions executed, and the curves the analytics plugins compute from the recorded ticks have fewer points. Two recordings of one run at different densities therefore give different tables of the same course.

### 6.2 Resources

What a run needs depends on its configuration. The default configuration that comes with Evochora is kept small on purpose, so that it runs on most machines: an environment of 2048 × 1152 cells with a population of under a hundred organisms, recorded at every ten-thousandth tick. The figures below refer to it. On a current laptop with eight cores it runs about 3 × 10⁹ ticks a day, 10⁶ to 10⁷ instructions per second. With the default ancestor, a program of some thousand cells, that is several thousand generations; a smaller ancestor copies itself in fewer ticks and yields more. The run needs about two gigabytes of memory, and its recording grows by about 150 gigabytes a day. The environments behind the published records were ten to a hundred times larger. Time, memory and disk depend on different things.

#### Time

Every living organism executes one instruction per tick, so the time a tick takes grows with the population. A larger environment with more energy sustains thousands of organisms, and a day then yields proportionally fewer ticks. How many generations those ticks are depends on the ancestor. A generation of the ancestors that come with Evochora takes 10⁵ to 10⁶ ticks, measured in the recorded runs as the mean age of a parent at a birth. A population of several thousand organisms thus still comes to several hundred generations a day. A small population yields many generations and strong drift; the published runs traded generations for populations of several hundred to over a thousand organisms.

#### Memory

Memory grows with the size of the environment and with how full it becomes. The largest environment behind a published record, 19200 × 12416 cells, ran on the same laptop. When a run starts, the node logs an estimate of what it will need and warns if the configured memory is lower; the estimate is conservative, and a run usually needs less than it says. How the memory is configured is in the [User Guide](USER_GUIDE.md).

#### Disk

The recording grows with the size of the environment, which bounds how many cells can be occupied, and with the density of the recording. With every ten-thousandth tick recorded, an environment of 7680 × 4320 cells took about 250 gigabytes for 3.5 × 10⁸ ticks. The largest, 19200 × 12416 cells, took about a terabyte for 10⁹ ticks. A denser recording grows in proportion, which is why a run is recorded sparsely and the stretches that matter are recorded again ([Record a Stretch in Full](USER_GUIDE.md#6-record-a-stretch-in-full)).

### 6.3 Direction

Evochora runs on one machine today. The recording and the indexing of a run are separate services behind replaceable interfaces, so moving them to other machines would mean new implementations of those interfaces, not a new design. Distributing the simulation itself, by partitioning the environment across nodes, is not designed. The direction such a partition would take is the one Ackley demonstrated with the Moveable Feast Machine [(Ackley, 2013)](#ref-ackley-2013). Organisms interact only with their neighborhood, so nodes would exchange little. It is named here as a direction, not a plan. Why Evochora is written in Java, with an assembly language of its own and as a set of services, is explained in [Architecture & Design Decisions](dev/ARCHITECTURE_DECISIONS.md).

## 8. Conclusion

Evochora is an open-source digital evolution platform designed to make the physics of a simulated world a first-class experimental variable. Its architecture — spatially distributed code in a configurable n-dimensional grid, a two-axis thermodynamic economy, fuzzy label matching for mutation-resilient genomes, and a modular plugin system for mutation, resource distribution, and death mechanics — provides researchers with a flexible testbed for a range of evolutionary experiments.

The platform has demonstrated its viability through long-running simulations sustaining stable populations over billions of ticks. Four configurable mutation operators, label namespace rewriting, a defensive primordial organism, and a fully deterministic, resumable data pipeline provide the foundation for reproducible research.

The research directions outlined in this document — from replicator ecology and environmental heterogeneity to digital eukaryogenesis, multicellularity, and reaction-based chemistry — represent a broad program of testable hypotheses. Some are immediately accessible with the current implementation; others require extensions that the architecture is designed to support. Researchers, students, and engineers are invited to use this platform, challenge its assumptions, and contribute to its development. The source code, documentation, and community channels are available at [https://github.com/evochora/evochora](https://github.com/evochora/evochora).
